How to Import Used Construction Equipment from Turkey: A Buyer's Guide

Buying a used excavator, wheel loader, dozer, crane, or backhoe loader across borders is not simply a matter of agreeing on a machine price. The buyer must verify the equipment, define the commercial terms, meet the destination country's import rules, and organize transport without losing control of the documentation. This guide explains how to import used construction equipment from Turkey through a practical, risk-based process. GND Construction Equipment does not operate as a dealer with a fixed showroom inventory. After receiving a buyer's requirement, GND searches its international manufacturer and supplier network, checks suitable options, and prepares a project-specific offer. Availability, price, and delivery timing therefore depend on the current search and verification process.

Start with the destination-country import rules

Before requesting machines, ask a licensed customs broker or the relevant authority in the destination country whether used construction equipment can be imported and under what conditions. Rules may depend on equipment type, age, engine emissions, safety standards, origin, intended use, and whether the machine is road-registered. Some countries require pre-shipment inspection, conformity documents, special permits, cleaning certificates, or restrictions on used machinery.

The tariff classification should be confirmed rather than guessed from a general product description. An excavator, self-propelled crane, forklift, attachment, and spare part may fall under different customs headings. Classification affects duty, taxes, permits, and supporting documents. The importer should also confirm the customs value method and whether freight, insurance, commissions, or other charges are included in the taxable base.

For EU destinations, the importer or its representative generally needs the appropriate customs identification, and entry-safety and import declarations must be handled through the applicable systems. Other destinations have their own registrations and electronic processes. Regulations change, so this guide is not a substitute for current advice from the destination authority and customs broker.

Build a precise machine requirement

A useful inquiry describes the work rather than naming only a model. State the equipment type, required operating weight or capacity range, digging depth, lift height, bucket size, undercarriage or tire preference, attachments, auxiliary hydraulic needs, cab specification, and any engine-emissions requirement. Add the destination port, preferred shipping window, and whether the machine must fit a container or will travel as roll-on/roll-off or breakbulk cargo.

If you have a brand preference, explain whether it is driven by local dealer support, spare-parts commonality, operator familiarity, or fleet standardization. CAT, Komatsu, Volvo, JCB, and other manufacturers can be considered without treating one brand as universally superior. GND should not be understood as an authorized dealer for a mentioned brand unless that status is expressly and independently confirmed.

A clear specification prevents a low-priced but unusable machine from entering the shortlist. It also allows comparable offers: the same attachment package, documentation scope, inspection standard, delivery term, and shipping basis should be evaluated together.

Verify identity, ownership, and history

The machine's serial number must be visible and consistent across the data plate, chassis markings, invoice, and supporting records. Ask for ownership or sale documents that establish the seller's right to transfer the equipment. Any altered, unreadable, or inconsistent identifier should be resolved before payment.

Request maintenance records, service invoices, component replacement history, and information about the machine's previous application. Meter hours are useful but not conclusive. A low-hour excavator used continuously with a breaker may have more structural and hydraulic wear than a higher-hour unit maintained for lighter work.

Check whether there are liens, finance interests, export restrictions, or other claims that could prevent lawful transfer. The exact method depends on the parties and jurisdictions, so qualified legal and customs professionals may be needed for higher-value transactions.

Arrange an independent technical inspection

Photographs and a short video are screening tools, not a complete inspection. A competent inspector should see the machine from a cold start, confirm the serial number, examine fluids and leaks, and operate it until normal working temperature. The test should cover the engine, cooling system, hydraulics, transmission or travel system, brakes, steering, electrical functions, and safety devices.

For an excavator, inspect the undercarriage, slew bearing, boom, arm, bucket linkage, cylinders, pumps, and final drives. For a wheel loader, check the center articulation, axles, transmission, brakes, lift arms, bucket linkage, and tires. Welds, reinforcement plates, fresh paint, abnormal noise, fault codes, and uneven wear deserve explanation. Where practical, use oil analysis, pressure tests, electronic diagnostics, and measurements instead of relying only on visual impressions.

Test the machine under a representative load. A loader that raises an empty bucket or an excavator that cycles in the air has not demonstrated working performance. The inspection report should include dated photographs, video, identified defects, recommended repairs, and an estimate of near-term maintenance needs.

Agree on commercial terms and payment protection

The sales contract or pro forma invoice should identify the parties, machine, serial number, attachments, included documents, condition basis, price, currency, delivery term, inspection rights, payment milestones, and remedies if the machine materially differs from the agreement. Use an Incoterms rule with a named place or port and the correct edition, but remember that Incoterms allocate certain costs and risks; they do not replace the full sales contract or determine ownership by themselves.

Choose a payment structure proportionate to the transaction risk. Bank-supported instruments, staged payment after inspection, or a reputable escrow arrangement may offer more control than an unprotected full advance. Verify bank details through a trusted secondary channel before sending funds, especially if account instructions change. Each party should obtain its own legal, banking, tax, and compliance advice.

Prepare export and shipping documents

The final document set depends on the machine, route, Incoterms rule, and destination. It may include a commercial invoice, packing or equipment list, transport document, export customs declaration, origin documentation when applicable, insurance certificate, inspection report, and technical records. Turkish exports are declared to the competent customs administration, but the exporter and customs representative must determine the exact procedure for the transaction.

Machine dimensions and weight should be measured in transport configuration. Remove or secure loose attachments, lower the boom or mast as planned, protect cylinders and glass, isolate batteries where required, and document the loading condition. Used construction equipment may carry soil, plant matter, oil residue, or other contamination that triggers destination biosecurity or environmental controls. Cleaning requirements must be confirmed before shipment, not after arrival.

Select container, flat rack, roll-on/roll-off, or breakbulk shipping based on actual dimensions, weight, lifting points, port capability, route, and cargo value. Obtain a transport plan that identifies who handles inland haulage, permits, port operations, lashing, marine insurance, and destination unloading.

Manage arrival, customs clearance, and handover

Send draft documents to the destination customs broker before departure so discrepancies can be corrected. The machine description, serial number, value, weights, and consignee details should be consistent across the invoice, transport document, inspection report, and declaration. The buyer should be prepared for customs inspection, storage, demurrage, port handling, inland transport, and tax payment under the agreed responsibility split.

On arrival, inspect the machine before or immediately after unloading, subject to port rules. Compare its condition with the loading report, record transport damage, verify attachments, and notify the carrier or insurer within the required time if a claim may exist. Complete local registration, workplace inspection, operator certification, or conformity steps before putting the equipment into service.
